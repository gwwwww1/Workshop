About the Workshop

Rare diseases individually affect fewer than 1 in 2,000 people, yet collectively an estimated 300 million individuals across more than 6,000 distinct disorders, approximately 72% of which are genetic in origin. Fewer than 5% have an approved therapy, and patients often endure a diagnostic odyssey exceeding five years. Cohorts number tens to hundreds of patients, described by high-dimensional genomic, imaging, and clinical data whose relationships to outcomes are context-dependent and incompletely specified.

Contemporary AI rests on assumptions these settings routinely violate—abundant labeled data, stable task definitions, homogeneous distributions, and well-defined ground truth—motivating four central ML challenges: (i) generalizing to unseen rare diseases; (ii) learning robustly from small cohorts with noisy, incomplete, or weakly labeled clinical annotations; (iii) transferring knowledge across diseases that share genomic mechanisms but differ phenotypically; and (iv) Evaluation under limited and evolving reference standards.

Recent work demonstrates these challenges are tractable: knowledge graph approaches now support zero-shot drug repurposing across thousands of diseases; multi-agent language model frameworks are beginning to assist in rare disease diagnosis; and clinical narrative integration is improving connections between patient records and biomedical ontologies. Yet these efforts remain fragmented and rarely evaluated in the most data-scarce settings.
